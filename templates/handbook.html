Part I

Foundations

Four words that everything else depends on, and the direct answer to "what do I actually give the developer?"

CH 1 How it fits together

Read this first — every other chapter assumes these four words.

WordWhat it meansExample
Platform ownerYou. You run the server, create tenants, issue keys. You sign in at /admin.You
TenantA customer or organisation. Their people, keys, settings and logs are completely separate from every other tenant's."Acme Clinic"
API keyWhat an application uses to reach the API. Belongs to one tenant, carries a role.fk_live_9a3f…
UserA person enrolled inside a tenant, under a user_id you choose. Stored as maths, never photographs.alice

What "tenant" really means

A tenant is a sealed box. Creating one gives it its own template store, its own encryption key, its own signing identity, its own audit log and its own usage counters.

  • A key for tenant A cannot reach anything belonging to tenant B — not by accident, not by crafting a clever request. The isolation lives in the storage layout, not in a filter someone could forget to apply.
  • Deleting a tenant destroys its encryption key, making the data unrecoverable even from a backup. That is deliberate (Chapter 7) and dangerous.

Running this for a single organisation? You still have a tenant — there is just one. Nothing else changes.

CH 2 What you hand someone

Yes — nobody can touch the API until you give them something from the admin console. Exactly three things.

  1. The base URL of your deployment, e.g. https://verify.example.com
  2. An API key (fk_…), minted at /admin → Keys. Shown once and never again — the server keeps only a hash.
  3. The tenant id the key belongs to, so they know which box they are writing into.

Optionally two more: a signing secret if they want to prove a result really came from your server (Chapter 13), and a portal password if they should manage their own keys (Chapter 6).

Give out a verify key, not an admin key, unless they genuinely need to enrol people. A verify key can check identities and nothing else — it cannot enrol, delete, or read your user list. If it leaks, the damage is bounded. Details in Chapter 5.

Part II

Administration

For the platform owner. Setting up operators, customers, keys and limits — and ending a relationship cleanly.

CH 3 Admin accounts

Who is allowed to sign in to the console.

The console at /admin is where all of Part II happens. Before any accounts exist you can get in with a bootstrap password from the environment variable FACE_ADMIN_PASSWORD.

Create a real account immediately. Once at least one named account exists, the bootstrap password stops working. Until then, anyone who learns that one environment variable is a full administrator.

python manage_admins.py create alice        # prompts for a password
python manage_admins.py list
python manage_admins.py remove alice

Passwords are stored hashed in admins.json, never committed to source control. You can also add and remove operators from the console once signed in.

Named accounts matter for a second reason: the audit log records which operator did each thing (Chapter 23). With a shared password, the log can only ever say "admin".

CH 4 Creating a tenant

Setting up a new customer.

A tenant comes into existence the first time you mint a key for it — there is no separate "create" step to forget. What you control explicitly is its entitlement: the green light and the limits.

SettingWhat it does
enabledThe master switch. Off means every call from that tenant's keys is refused. Your paywall and your suspension lever.
planA label you choose, for your own billing records.
max_keysHow many keys they may hold. Minting past it is refused — including in their own portal.
allowed_rolesWhich roles they may mint. Restrict a customer to verify if they should never enrol.
allow_exportWhether they may export provisioning bundles and credential data.
palm_enabledWhether palm works for them at all, or face only.
match_policyHow strict matching is for this tenant.

All on one console screen. Changes take effect immediately — no restart, no redeploy.

Two settings the tenant controls

  • Allowed browser origins — the web addresses their browser app may call from (Chapter 15).
  • A webhook URL — optional; receives every enrol, verify and identify event.

CH 5 Issuing API keys

The thing you actually hand over.

RoleMayGive it to
verifyVerify and identify people.Door terminals, kiosks, phones — anything physically out in the world.
adminEverything: enrol, verify, delete, manage settings, and the subsystems in Chapter 24.Their backend. Their registration desk.

In the console: Keys → New key. Name it something you will recognise later ("Acme kiosk fleet"), pick the tenant and role. Or from a terminal:

python manage_keys.py create "Acme App" --tenant acme
python manage_keys.py list
python manage_keys.py revoke acme

The raw key appears exactly once. Copy it straight into wherever it is going. The server stores only a hash, so there is no "show it again" — you revoke and mint another.

Batches and revocation

For a customer rolling out twenty kiosks, bulk mint gives you 1 admin + 20 verify keys as one download. Each is separate, so you can revoke one bad kiosk without disturbing the other nineteen. Revoking is instant; revoking by tenant kills all their keys at once.

CH 6 Tenant self-service

Letting a customer manage themselves.

You need not be the bottleneck for every key. Give a tenant a portal password and they sign in at /portal.

  1. Set their password in the console: Tenants → Portal access. Minimum six characters.
  2. Send them /portal and the password, ideally not by email.
  3. They manage themselves — minting keys, checking usage, adjusting settings.

They stay inside the limits you set in Chapter 4. Capped at five keys, the portal refuses the sixth exactly as your console would — the entitlement is enforced in one place, so the two views cannot disagree.

↑ Contents

CH 7 Offboarding a tenant

Ending a customer relationship, permanently.

One button does all of this: revokes every key, revokes every outstanding invite, deletes the entire template store, destroys the encryption key, and removes the signing identity, issued credentials, devices, policies and settings.

This is a crypto-erase, not a delete. Because the encryption key is destroyed, the data is unrecoverable even from a backup taken five minutes earlier. Exactly what you want for a right-to-erasure request, and exactly what you do not want if you clicked the wrong row. There is no undo.

To merely suspend someone — unpaid invoice, contract dispute — set enabled to off instead. Everything stops, nothing is destroyed, and flipping it back restores them completely.

Building on the API

For the integrating developer. This is the part to forward when someone asks "how do I use this in my app?"

CH 8 Quick start

Three calls cover most integrations.

# 1. Enrol - one or more captures of the same person
curl -X POST https://your-host/v1/enroll \
  -H "X-API-Key: fk_yourkey" -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -d '{"user_id": "alice", "images": ["<base64>", "<base64>"]}'

# 2. Verify a claim - "is this alice?"  (1:1)
curl -X POST https://your-host/v1/verify \
  -H "X-API-Key: fk_yourkey" \
  -d '{"user_id": "alice", "image": "<base64>"}'

# 3. Or identify - "who is this?"  (1:N, omit user_id)
curl -X POST https://your-host/v1/identify \
  -H "X-API-Key: fk_yourkey" -d '{"image": "<base64>"}'

Every endpoint except /v1/health needs X-API-Key. Images are base64; a data: prefix is accepted and stripped.

from faceverify import FaceVerifyClient
fv = FaceVerifyClient("https://your-host", "fk_yourkey")
fv.enroll("alice", ["a1.jpg", "a2.jpg"])
r = fv.verify("alice", "probe.jpg")
if r["success"]: print("granted via", r["modality"])

Sandbox keys return realistic responses without touching the model or storage — point your test suite at one and it will never create a real biometric record.

CH 9 Face or palm — you never say which

The one idea that keeps the rest of the API small.

You do not tell the server which it is. It detects that per image and routes accordingly.

  • One user_id can hold a face and a palm. Presenting either verifies them — a match is a match.
  • One person may enrol both palms (capped at two, because people have two). Either hand works.
  • A single photo containing a face and an open palm enrols both.

The response says which modality decided, in modality. You can pin it with "modality": "face" or "palm", but the default is to let the server decide.

Why it matters beyond convenience: someone wearing a face covering, or whose face is hard to capture in harsh sun, is still served — without your app branching on which body part is available.

CH 10 Two ways to integrate

Decide who is allowed to hold biometric data.

Managed — the server stores templates

You send images and a user_id; the server keeps the template and matches. Simplest to build.

POST/v1/enroll
POST/v1/verify
POST/v1/identify
GET/v1/users

Stateless — you store everything

Nothing is persisted server-side. You get a vector, keep it in your own database, send it back to compare.

POST/v1/embed→ embedding[]
POST/v1/compareprobe vs references[]

An embedding is not a photograph and you cannot rebuild a face from it — but it is biometric data under most privacy law. Store it as carefully as you would the photo.

CH 11 Core endpoints

POST /v1/enroll
{ "user_id": "alice", "images": ["<base64>", …],
  "expires_in_days": 30 }               // optional: a guest, see Ch 20

{ "success": true, "enrolled": 2, "of": 2, "results": [ … ] }

Compare enrolled with of. "2 of 3" is normal — one blurry frame rejected, the others fine — and still a usable enrolment. results explains each rejection so you can coach the person: hold steadier, more light, move closer.

Verify (1:1) vs identify (1:N)

Pass user_id to ask "is this that person?". Omit it to ask "who is this?" across everyone enrolled.

Identify is genuinely harder: the person must beat every other enrolled person, not merely clear a bar. Expect it to grow more conservative as the population grows. Prefer verify wherever your app already knows who the person claims to be — a card, a booking, a typed name.

GET/v1/userswho is enrolled
POST/v1/users/deleteremove one person
POST/v1/users/purgebulk removal
POST/v1/users/exporteverything held on one person

CH 12 Bursts & liveness

Sending several frames instead of one.

  • Palm — the sharpest usable frame is chosen, so one soft or blurred frame does not sink the attempt.
  • Face — with active liveness on, the burst is checked against a head-turn challenge, defeating someone holding up a photo.
GET  /v1/challenge          // -> { token, instruction }
POST /v1/verify  { "user_id": "alice", "frames": [ … ], "token": "…" }

Tokens expire. A liveness error means "get a fresh token and retry", not "this person is a fraud" — word your interface accordingly.

CH 13 Trusting a result

{ "success": true, "user_id": "alice", "score": 0.83,
  "signature": { "alg": "HMAC-SHA256", "ts": 1786…, "nonce": "…", "hmac": "…" } }

If a decision travels from somewhere you do not control — a phone, a kiosk, a browser — to your backend, check the signature server-side. Otherwise anyone who can post JSON at your endpoint can simply claim they were granted access. The SDKs expose verify_signature(response); the timestamp and nonce let you reject replays.

CH 14 Errors & retries

{ "success": false, "code": "no_face",
  "message": "No face detected. Move closer, with light on your face." }

message is written to be shown to the person at the camera — it says what to do next. code is what your application branches on. Never branch on message; the wording will change.

SituationHTTPWhat to do
Capture unusable — no_face, no_hand, blur400Show the message, recapture. Not the person's fault.
Not recognised200success: false. A normal outcome, not an HTTP error.
Recognised but not permitted200Guest expired, consent withdrawn, policy denied — see Chapter 18.
Wrong key for the job403A verify key used for an admin action.
Bad or revoked key401Check the key, and that the tenant is still enabled.
Too many requests429Back off and retry — the SDKs do this for you.
Body too large413Downscale before sending; 1280px on the long edge is plenty.

A denial is an HTTP 200. The request succeeded; the answer was no. Treating any non-200 as "denied" will make your integration behave strangely the first time a camera sends a blurry frame.

Retrying safely

Send an Idempotency-Key header on writes. The first response for that key is remembered for 24 hours and replayed on retry, so a network timeout cannot enrol the same person twice.

CH 15 Browsers, webhooks & the widget

Calling from a browser

You can call the API straight from a web page, but the tenant must first register the origin (e.g. https://app.acme.com) in their settings, or the browser blocks it. The API key still governs what the caller may do — origin registration only controls which pages may ask.

Webhooks

Set a webhook URL and every enrol, verify and identify event is posted to it as signed JSON. Delivery is best-effort on a background thread; it never blocks or fails the original call. This is the only outbound network call the system makes — with no webhook set, a deployment never phones home at all.

The drop-in widget

For a site that just needs a verification box, /widget.js gives you one without writing capture code.

CH 17 Invite links

Self-enrolment without handing out the admin password.

An invite is a private link letting one named person enrol themselves, once, on their own device. It answers "how do I enrol two hundred students without sitting with each one?"

  1. Create the invite. Console → Invites → New. Type the person's name or id, optionally how long the link lives.
  2. Send the link. Message, email, printed QR — whatever reaches them.
  3. They enrol themselves. The page walks them through capturing face and/or palm. No password, no operator.
  4. The link burns. Once finished it cannot be reused.

The identity comes from the link, never from a form field. The person cannot choose or type who they are — the invite fixed it when you created it. That is what makes unsupervised enrolment safe: they can prove their biometric, but cannot claim someone else's name.

Invites can be generated in bulk for a whole list, revoked while unused, and can optionally issue a QR credential at the end. Someone already fully enrolled is refused a new invite — delete them first if you deliberately want to re-enrol, which stops a stray link quietly overwriting good biometrics.

Part V

Running it day to day

What happens after a match, and the moving parts an operator touches.

CH 18 After the match

Why a recognised person can still be turned away.

STEP 1BiometricIs this the person? Pure matching, nothing else considered.
STEP 2Guest expiryWas this a time-boxed identity that has run out?
STEP 3ConsentIs their consent still standing, or withdrawn?
STEP 4Access policyDo the rules admit them here, now?

The order is deliberate. The biometric decision is made first and in isolation, so it can be audited on its own terms and no policy change can quietly weaken matching. Policy is applied afterwards and can only narrow a grant, never widen one.

Do not tell a recognised-but-expired visitor "not recognised". Both return success: false, and only code distinguishes them. Getting this wrong makes people re-present their palm over and over at a door that was never going to open.

CH 19 Devices

Knowing which kiosks exist, and killing a lost one.

Without this, every kiosk shares one key: nothing records which physical device did what, and cutting off a stolen one means rotating the whole fleet.

  1. Mint a pairing code in the console — single-use, fifteen minutes.
  2. Enter it on the device. It redeems the code and receives its own verify key and signing secret, shown once.
  3. It checks in periodically, so the console shows which devices are alive and when each was last seen.
  4. Rename or disable any device. Disabling revokes that one device's key immediately, leaving every other device working.

Because the device authenticates with its own key, there is no device id anyone could spoof — the key is the identity.

CH 20 Guests — identities that expire

Nothing in the store ages on its own: enrol someone once and they verify forever. Right for staff, wrong for visitors, contractors, exam candidates and event passes.

Add expires_in_days or expires_in_hours at enrolment and that person becomes a guest. After expiry, verification returns identity_expired instead of a grant. The template is untouched — expiry is a separate gate checked after matching (Chapter 18). Purge expired guests in bulk from the console when you want the records actually gone.

↑ Contents

CH 21 Guardians — verifying for someone else

In welfare distribution, clinics and schools the same case recurs: the entitled person cannot present a biometric — a child, an elderly parent, a patient. The usual options are to turn them away or abandon verification entirely.

A guardianship link is a third option: an admin explicitly links a guardian to a beneficiary, and the record is audited.

POST /v1/verify
{ "user_id": "mary", "on_behalf_of": "child_ama", "image": "<base64>" }

Mary presents her own face or palm. The biometric decision is hers; the entitlement checks run against the beneficiary. Nothing is weakened — an impostor cannot collect for Ama, because they would still have to be Mary.

CH 22 Access policies

Verification answers "who is this?". A policy answers "and are they allowed here, now?" — opening hours, groups, which door.

Policies are evaluated strictly after the biometric decision, so they can never touch thresholds, liveness or routing. A policy can only narrow an already-granted match. Manage groups and rules in the console or through /v1/policies. For far more specialised rules — shift rosters, anti-passback, escorts, occupancy caps — see Chapter 25.

↑ Contents

CH 23 Audit, usage & quotas

Audit log — every meaningful action is recorded: enrolments, deletions, key minting, entitlement changes, offboarding, device changes. Each entry names the operator, which is the real reason to give every admin their own account (Chapter 3).

Usage and quotas — billable calls (enrol, verify, identify, embed, compare) are counted per tenant per calendar month. Show a customer their usage, or cap it.

Duplicate detection — at enrolment the platform checks whether this face or palm is already enrolled under a different name and refuses if so. That is what stops one person quietly holding two identities, which matters anywhere entitlements are handed out per person.

CH 24 Calling a subsystem

One door to 166 subsystems and 851 operations. Requires an admin key.

Rather than 851 hand-written endpoints, the subsystems are mounted generically. Three calls give you discovery and execution:

GET/v1/servicescatalogue: every subsystem + summary
GET/v1/services/<name>its functions and their arguments
POST/v1/services/<name>/<function>call it; JSON body = arguments

Discover what something takes, then call it:

# What can the locker subsystem do?
curl -H "X-API-Key: fk_admin" https://your-host/v1/services/lockers

# Assign locker 12 to alice
curl -X POST https://your-host/v1/services/lockers/assign \
  -H "X-API-Key: fk_admin" -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -d '{"locker_id": "12", "user_id": "alice"}'

# -> { "success": true, "service": "lockers",
#      "function": "assign", "result": { … } }

You never pass the tenant. It is taken from your API key and injected. A body containing tenant is rejected outright rather than ignored, so nobody can read or write another tenant's data by guessing a parameter name.

Other guarantees worth knowing: only public functions on mounted subsystems are callable, everything needs the manage scope (an admin key), and unknown arguments are refused with a message pointing at the discovery endpoint rather than failing obscurely.

CH 26 Gates, and trying one safely

44 subsystems can refuse a person who genuinely matched. None is switched on.

A gate takes a verification result and may turn success into refusal — "yes that is Ama, but she is off-shift", "yes that is Kwame, but he is already inside". They are the mechanism behind Chapter 18.

None of the 44 is wired into live verification. Adding a gate changes who gets through a door, so it stays a deliberate act rather than something that happens because a subsystem exists. Nothing in Part VI can currently refuse anybody.

Rehearsing a gate

Before adopting one, see what it would decide without it deciding anything:

POST /v1/services/occupancy/gate_preview
{ "result": { "success": true, "user_id": "alice" }, "direction": "in" }

// -> { "before": {…}, "after": {…}, "would_change": true }

Feed it the match you expect and read would_change. If a gate would have turned away half your staff on day one, you find that out here rather than at the door.

Part VII

Working offline

The part that matters where there is no signal: credentials on paper, air-gapped devices, and phones that never talk to a server.

CH 27 QR credentials

Proving identity with no network at all.

A credential is a signed QR carrying the person's own protected template. A verifier checks it completely offline — no server, no signal, no lookup.

  1. Issue it from the console, the API, or automatically at the end of an invite enrolment.
  2. The person keeps it — on their phone, or printed. Paper matters: it works with no battery.
  3. A verifier scans it at /verify-credential or in the Android app, and captures the person live.
  4. The check runs locally: signature, then expiry, then revocation, then the live capture against the template inside the QR.
  • Issuer keys — each tenant has its own signing identity. Rotating retires the old private key but keeps the public half, so credentials already issued keep verifying.
  • Trust store — the issuers a verifier accepts, so an offline device can check a credential from another organisation.
  • Revocation — revoked credentials ship inside the trust store, so a cancelled credential stops working even on a device that has never been online since.
  • Wallet card — /card renders it in a form the person can save or print.

CH 28 Air-gapped devices

Provisioning bundles — the air-gapped Android build never talks to a server. To load it with people, export a bundle: a passphrase-encrypted file of templates, never images. Move it by USB or device management and import it with the same passphrase. No network path is ever opened; the bundle is the only thing that crosses, and it is useless without the passphrase.

Sync — the hybrid build can pull a dataset copy and push back what it recorded offline, reconciling later. For sites where connectivity exists but cannot be relied on.

Glance index — an exported index for fast local lookup without the full store.

Part VIII

Privacy & compliance

Biometric data is special-category data almost everywhere. This is what the platform gives you to handle that properly.

CH 30 Consent & data-subject rights

Encryption protects the data; consent records establish that the person agreed — which is what an auditor asks for and what the person can later withdraw.

  • Each tenant registers the consent statement they show people, with a version.
  • Consent is recorded automatically at enrolment, against whichever version was current.
  • A person can withdraw it. From then on their matches stop being granted — enforced in the pipeline (Chapter 18), not just noted in a file.
They askYou use
"What do you hold on me?"/v1/users/export, or they visit /my-data themselves
"Delete me."/v1/users/delete
"I withdraw consent."/v1/consent/withdraw
"Delete our whole organisation."Tenant offboarding — Chapter 7

Template protection

Stored templates are kept in a protected, cancelable form. If one ever leaked it can be reissued: the person keeps their face and palm, but the stored maths changes and the leaked copy stops matching. A password can be changed after a breach; a face cannot — this stands in for that. Reissuing also revokes that person's QR credentials, since whatever leak prompted it taints those too.

APX B Health & reference

GET/v1/healthno key needed — for load balancers
GET/readyzis the model loaded, and which matching settings are live
GET/v1/openapi.yamlthe full machine-readable contract
GET/v1/servicesthe live subsystem catalogue

Point any OpenAPI client generator at /v1/openapi.yaml to generate a client in your language.

/readyz reports the matching settings actually in force — useful because an environment variable can be set on a container running an image too old to understand it, and everything will still look healthy.

↑ Contents

Every endpoint except /v1/health requires an X-API-Key header. Tenants are fully isolated; keys carry roles; verification results are signed. The machine-readable contract lives at /v1/openapi.yaml and the live subsystem catalogue at /v1/services on your own deployment.
